Put it this way: when the ancient Romans spoke, they were using two languages at once.One was spoken aloud, the other was silent.The messages carried by these two languages could be identical, complementary, or completely different.Quintilianpointed out that gesture can convey meaning without words, forming a complete language in itself.An orator can—and must—master this language as powerful proof of linguistic command. He declared:

A mediocre speech, if reinforced by the power of delivery (bodily action), will impress more than the finest speech lacking that power (bodily action).

Quintilian held that the gestures of the deaf replace speech; for the orator, gestures possess value as well.Cicerobelievedthat 'the language of the body' (sermo corporis)was no less important than spoken delivery.

On this topic, several rhetorical handbooks from around the turn of the era survive, the most important being Quintilian’s extraordinarily detailedInstitutio Oratoriaand Cicero’s three works:De Oratore, Brutus, and Orator.

The centerpiece of body language was, of course, gesture. Quintilian describes specific hand and arm movements for expressing every emotion. He lists nearly twenty gestures, including subtle variations on basic finger positions.Some finger placements are simple, such as raising the hand with fingers slightly curled, a gesture that accompanies exhortation.

Gesture used in exhortation

Others are highly complex: touching the upper joint of the index finger with the tips of thumb and middle finger, while bending the remaining two fingers slightly toward the palm without touching it, the little finger curved more sharply than its neighbors.

Gesture used in presenting an argument

Some of these emotional gestures seem natural, almost universal. Clenching the hand and pressing it to the chest expresses intense grief or anger.Quintilian remarks that an orator striking this pose might cry, 'What am I to do now?' in despair.

Gesture expressing anger or sorrow

Some gestures seem to draw their meaning from cultural beliefs or attitudes.For instance, to express fear of a person or an idea, the speaker was told to turn both palms toward the left.Because the left hand or left side usually carries negative connotations, these notions may be linked to this gesture.

Gesture signifying panic

By the same logic, to show disgust the right hand is stretched to the left, the left shoulder thrust forward, and the head turned to the right.

Gesture signifying disgust

Here the emotional meaning of “disgust” is conveyed by the speaker’s body moving away from the left side. Conversely, to express adoration the speaker can stretch both arms toward the person or object revered.

Gesture signifying adoration or supplication

Other meanings appear more arbitrary, such as bringing the hand to the mouth while pinching the fingertips together; Quintilian says this should denote slight surprise, indignation, or entreaty.

Gesture signifying slight surprise, indignation, or entreaty

Ideally the speaker’s body language should stir the appropriate emotions in the audience, making them receptive to the message.Even Cicero admitted he was easily swayed by a practiced gesturer; he once confessed to another orator, “The movement of your fingers made me tremble with excitement.”

All rhetorical handbooks warn against overly violent or exaggerated motions that can make the speaker lose control and appear inelegant. Quintilian lists examples of speakers who overused gestures and remarks that standing next to them was dangerous, since their sudden, violent movements might strike bystanders.In general, gestures should be fairly restrained, performed chiefly by the right arm and hand, while the left is used only for special emphasis. The left hand should never act alone but should cooperate with the right, and movements from the speaker’s left to right are considered most graceful.Moreover, the arms should move only between the waist and the eyes.Yet several gestures that exceed these limits are still approved, such as outstretched arms in prayer or the right arm raised in exhortation.For the latter, it is acceptable so long as the speaker does not heighten the effect by letting the open hand quiver. Quintilian calls such trembling a theatrical trick unsuitable for an orator. In his view, dramatic gestures involving larger bodily movements or extending beyond the speaker’s torso are best reserved for“important subjects, scenes of joy, or moments of terror.” 。

Many gestures are static: once the desired position is formed, the fingers and arm are held perfectly rigid.Other gestures require finger movement as an essential part of the signal; without it the expression is incomplete. This is true of the gesture for “wonder.” The speaker raises the right hand with all fingers extended, then curls them one by one into the palm, starting with the little finger. When all fingers are closed, they are opened again in reverse order while the whole hand is gently rotated.

Gesture signifying wonder

Some gestures combine a static finger position with movement of the entire arm. To express humility the speaker brings the tips of the thumb and three other fingers together and moves the hand toward the mouth or chest. Once the hand nears the mouth, the speaker relaxes the hand and arm, letting them drop toward the ground.

Gesture signifying modesty or self-deprecation

Overall, movements are divided into six types: forward, backward, right, left, upward, and downward, each carrying its own meaning, though these meanings are not specified in detail.Sometimes the meaning of a gesture can shift depending on the context in which it appears.For instance, lightly cupping the palm and touching the fingertips to the chest can signal advice, reproach, or sympathy, depending on the scene. The move seems reserved for special moments, since Quintilian warns that an orator should use it sparingly, only ‘on the rarest occasions’.

Gestures for advice, blame, or praise

Although a speaker’s movements should be restrained, too little action betrays a lack of emotional engagement—considered a serious fault. Cicero rebukes one orator for insufficient gesture:

‘As for you, Marcus Calidius… there was no sign of agitation in either your heart or your body.’‘Did you strike your forehead? Did you slap your thigh, or at least stamp your foot?’‘In fact, instead of stirring any emotion in me, you almost sent me straight to sleep.’

The passage links gesture to feeling. Indeed, Cicero even praises sudden, violent actions—stamping, striking the head—because such theatrical moves are especially good at arousing the audience’s emotions.

The system’s complexity is also shown by the fact thatseveral different gestures can express the same emotion, yet they normally convey differing intensities of it.The speaker must match the emotional content of the words to the appropriate gesture—one that not only captures the right feeling but also communicates the required nuance of intensity.Moreover, every motion must stay in precise sync with the rhythm of the language.The hand should begin its movement at the start of the corresponding sentence or phrase and finish exactly as the words end, lest an awkward gap arise between what is said and what is shown. Orators are warned neither to let the hand anticipate the words nor to let the words outrun the hand.

Another staple gesture is the most basic human motion of all—pointing. Roman speakers routinely pointed to themselves, to objects, to places, or to the person under discussion.A simple form is to gesture toward the chest without touching it when referring to oneself; pointing straight at another person is also acceptable.Speakers likewise use indicative motions for things and locations. The Roman pointing sign is the same as ours: index finger extended, the rest curled under the thumb.

Gestures for pointing to people, ground, or objects

Yet pointing downward adds emphasis, while a modified point over the shoulder signals agreement.Pointing with the thumb is deemed unbecoming for an orator, though Quintilian admits the vulgar habit is widespread. He notes that speakers use the pointing gesture in place of adverbs and pronouns to indicate places and persons.

The speech also contains hand and arm movements tied to specific parts: exordium, argument, praise, entreaty, even denunciation. The gesture for the exordium is counted among the commonest; the middle finger meets the thumb while the other three fingers stretch out, and the hand glides gently forward while swaying left and right.

Gesture for opening a topic / exordium

This gesture embodies the sophistication of Roman body language.By varying the speed and amplitude of a gesture, the same movement can carry multiple meanings or serve different purposes.For instance, if the speaker accelerates the opening-gesture and extends the arm farther, it becomes the gesture for stating a fact; performed with even greater force and speed, it turns into the proper movement for refuting or rebuking an opponent. This tactic of tweaking velocity to convey slightly different shades of meaning allows a handful of basic gestures to cover a wider semantic range with greater flexibility.

A related gesture is the one used while stating facts: the speaker forms a circle with thumb and forefinger while stretching the remaining three fingers outward.

Gesture for stating facts

If those three fingers are folded into the palm, the same movement suits the delivery of enthymemes.

Gesture for enthymemes

When presenting an argument, the orator should pinch the outermost joint of the index finger between thumb and middle finger, letting the last two fingers curve slightly. A more emphatic version draws those final two fingers even tighter. Just as rhetorical theory prescribes fixed parts for every speech, it also assigns a distinctive gesture to each part. Thus, as the oration unfolds, its progress can be read not only in the verbal structure but also in the shifting repertoire of hand movements.

It must be noted that the “body language” mentioned at the outset is not confined to the hands; posture, the suitability of any movement, and the emotion each motion conveys are strictly codified for every part of the body.Among these, the head receives considerable attention:

  • Eyes:The speaker’s gaze must betray neither lewdness nor lethargy, nor stare vacantly or wander distractedly. To harness the eyes’ full power, one must not squint: “no one but an idiot speaks with closed eyes.”
  • Eyebrows:Avoid unseemly twitching or raising one higher than the other; their position can signal anger, sorrow, or joy.
  • Neck:Keep it upright, neither stiff nor thrown back. As for the throat, both stretching and contracting are equally unbecoming: elongation weakens and tires the voice, while a lowered chin renders it muffled and harsh. Shrugging or hunching the shoulders is generally wrong, for it shortens the neck and suggests servility and deceit.
  • Nose:Flaring the nostrils, wrinkling them, snorting in anger or contempt, widening them deliberately, or flattening them with the hand are all vulgar; even blowing the nose too often is censured.
  • Lips:Pouting, smacking them open suddenly, compressing or drawing them back to bare the teeth, twisting them sideways toward the ear, curling them in scorn, letting them droop, or allowing the voice to escape from only one corner are ugly habits; licking or biting them is likewise improper.

Roman rhetoricians normally divided an oration into five parts: inventio, collocatio, elocutio, memoria, and actio. Actio itself was formally defined as comprising two elements—vocal tone and gesture. Hence it is clear that gesture was an integral component of ancient delivery. As the Greek orator Demosthenes said:“The first requisite of rhetoric is delivery, the second is delivery, and the third is delivery.”
