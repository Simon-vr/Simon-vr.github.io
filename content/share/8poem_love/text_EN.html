I want to start from Nineteen Old Poems to discuss why there are so many love chapters in it. The Book of Songs is a moral textbook, and primary and secondary school textbooks are also moral textbooks. But Nineteen Old Poems is not. No country's moral textbook would say "The day is short while the bitter night is long - why not go wandering by candle light?" Only "Marching On and On" and "Far, Far Away the Cowherd Star" were selected for textbooks, as their themes are relatively traditional and do not sting mainstream values.

Since The Book of Songs, love has been incorporated into social order to maintain the family. The "love" sung in The Book of Songs is not a private experience, but a part of social function. Textbooks hope we will "be faithful to one partner for life" and domesticate love into order. But Nineteen Old Poems is different. It breaks away from the ethical perspective and writes about emotions and existence from the individual standpoint.

Therefore, let's try to jump out of the "moral textbook" framework to appreciate the full depth of "the crown of five-character poetry" and think why love is held in such high esteem.

Let's start with "Today's Grand Banquet". This poem most notably abandons moral judgment:

Today we hold a splendid feast,
With joys beyond all telling.
The zither strikes its free and easy notes,
The new melodies are divinely beautiful.
The virtuous sing lofty words,
Those who understand music hear the true meaning.
All hearts share the same wish,
Yet no one dares to speak their mind.
Man's life in this world is but a sojourn,
Swift as dust blown by a whirlwind.
Why not spur your swift steed,
And seize the important positions first?
Do not cling to poverty and lowliness,
And suffer hardship and misfortune all your life.

The author of "Today's Grand Banquet" realizes the brevity of life: "Man's life in this world is but a sojourn, swift as dust blown by a whirlwind." His prescription is secular and utilitarian: "Why not spur your swift steed, and seize the important positions first? Do not cling to poverty and lowliness, and suffer hardship and misfortune all your life." He clearly proposes that since life is short, we must use it efficiently to pursue fame and fortune.

"Today's Grand Banquet" can unhesitatingly say "Why not spur your swift steed, and seize the important positions first." The poet completely sets aside moral judgment and only considers two questions:

  1. How to make the most effective use of this "dust-like" lifetime? Not for the country, but for oneself.
  2. Is fame and fortune an efficient tool?

"Green, Green, the Cypress on the Mound" then questions the "spur your swift steed" prescription of "Today's Grand Banquet".

Green, green the cypress on the mound,
Piled stones in the mountain streams.
Between heaven and earth man's life rushes past,
Like a traveler on a distant journey.
With a cup of wine let us amuse ourselves,
Value it highly, do not think it too meager.
I drive my carriage, whip the slow horses,
And roam for pleasure to Wan and Luo.
How bustling is Luoyang!
Officials and nobles seek each other out.
Long avenues lined with narrow alleys,
Princes and marquises have many mansions.
The two palaces face each other from afar,
Their twin towers rise over a hundred feet.
Feast to your heart's content,
Why be oppressed by sorrow and anxiety?

The opening lines "Green, green the cypress on the mound, piled stones in the mountain streams" — eternal natural scenery contrasts with "Between heaven and earth man's life rushes past, like a traveler on a distant journey" — the transient life of a sojourner. The poet first tries to relieve himself with "a cup of wine to amuse ourselves", then "drives his carriage, whips the slow horses, and roams for pleasure to Wan and Luo", actively entering the center of wealth and fame — Luoyang — to examine the ultimate result of "spurring your swift steed".

He sees the prosperity of Luoyang: "Officials and nobles seek each other out", "Princes and marquises have many mansions", and the power center of "the two palaces facing each other from afar".

He discovers that even in this place of "feasting to your heart's content", the center of power and the pinnacle of material abundance, people still cannot escape the sorrow of "why be oppressed by sorrow and anxiety".

The poet sees that although princes and marquises "feast to their heart's content", they are essentially still "travelers" who ultimately cannot escape the limitations of life. Therefore, he sighs that this endogenous and universal anxiety cannot be dispelled by external prosperity.

One view holds that the poet felt grief and indignation when seeing the extravagant life of the nobles in Luoyang, worrying about the peril of the country, and thus uttered the exclamation "why be oppressed by sorrow and anxiety". But this contradicts the opening line "Between heaven and earth man's life rushes past, like a traveler on a distant journey". If the theme were "worrying about the country", the opening should be scenes of "national anxiety", not sighs about "the brevity of life".

If the poet were really expressing "concern for the country and the people" or satire against the nobles, he could have used the direct imagery of criticizing the current situation common in Han Yuefu or later Du Fu's poems, such as criticizing official corruption, people's destitution, heavy taxes, describing frontier beacon fires, displaced people, lost territory, or directly condemning the luxurious behavior of nobles.

However, what the poet writes is "How bustling is Luoyang! Officials and nobles seek each other out. Long avenues lined with narrow alleys, princes and marquises have many mansions. The two palaces face each other from afar, their twin towers rise over a hundred feet." The scene here is only prosperity, the concentration of power and wealth, without any direct negative moral judgment, let alone signs of national decline. He is just calmly observing an extreme form of secular achievement.

Nobles are those who "feast to their heart's content", the representatives who have "spurred their swift steeds" to the summit. The poet observes that even having reached the pinnacle of secular achievement in life, they still "are oppressed by sorrow and anxiety".

This precisely illustrates that the fundamental human anxiety (death, nothingness) cannot be eliminated by any external success or wealth. This anxiety torments every "traveler" equally, whether a prince or an ordinary scholar.

This is also a common theme in Chinese poetry. Li He's "The Kunlun Messenger" is the same:

No message comes from the Kunlun messenger,
The misty trees of Maoling wear a hue of sorrow.
The golden plate drips with jade dew endlessly,
Yet the boundless primal energy cannot be gathered.
The stone patterns on the qilin's back are cracked,
The red branches beneath the dragon's scales are broken.
What place particularly wounds the hearts of all nations?
The bright moon hangs high in the midnight sky.

Emperor Wu of Han was the pinnacle of both "spurring the swift steed" and "seeking immortality". He not only possessed the highest secular power but also exhausted the resources of the entire country to seek immortality ("No message comes from the Kunlun messenger", "The golden plate drips with jade dew endlessly"). His failure ("Yet the boundless primal energy cannot be gathered") is the ultimate failure of humanity's struggle against time.

And the last two lines "What place particularly wounds the hearts of all nations? The bright moon hangs high in the midnight sky" transcend the ordinary perspective of "mocking the emperor's extravagance and foolishness". "Why be oppressed by sorrow and anxiety", "particularly wounds the hearts of all nations" — this is the sorrow that exists universally in the hearts of nobles and scholars, which cannot be eliminated by secular forces and transcends class. The hanging "bright moon" quietly overlooks the transience of the human world. Before eternity, there is no difference between an emperor and a commoner.

Then, "The Years of Life Reach Not One Hundred" and "I Ride My Chariot from the Upper East Gate" propose another prescription — carpe diem.

The years of life reach not one hundred,
Yet the heart is in constant worry for a millennium.
The day is short while the bitter night is long —
Why not go wandering by candle light?
Seek joy while you may,
How can you wait for the coming year?
Fools hoard their wealth,
Only to be laughed at by later generations.
The immortal Prince Wang Ziqiao —
We can hardly hope to equal him.

The poet feels that the length of "life not reaching one hundred" cannot be changed, but he is unwilling to accept it. He wants to gain more life experience and happy time within the limited length of life. He provides the most radical "antidote" for the brevity of life — simply abandon all long-term plans and moral constraints and enjoy the present to the extreme.

"I Ride My Chariot from the Upper East Gate" is highly similar in conception and conclusion to "The Years of Life Reach Not One Hundred".

I drive my chariot through the Upper East Gate,
And gaze far at the graves north of the city.
How sadly the white poplars rustle,
Pines and cypresses line the broad road.
Beneath lie the dead of old,
In endless night they sleep forever.
Hidden in slumber beneath the Yellow Springs,
For a thousand years they will never wake.
The endless cycle of yin and yang turns,
Man's life is like morning dew.
Our life is but a brief sojourn,
It cannot last as long as metal and stone.
For ten thousand ages people send each other off,
Even sages and worthies cannot escape.
Taking elixirs to seek immortality —
Many are poisoned by the wrong medicines.
Better to drink fine wine,
And wear silk and satin clothes.

This time, the poet directly places himself at the scene of death: instead of meditating indoors, he "drives his chariot through the Upper East Gate" and directly faces "the graves north of the city", listening to the mournful rustle of "the white poplars". This makes the feeling of death no longer abstract, but tangible coldness and eternal silence ("In endless night they sleep forever", "For a thousand years they will never wake").

Perhaps because his description of the death scene is material, correspondingly, his description of life's pleasures is also material, not the vague "wandering by candle light", but "drink fine wine, and wear silk and satin clothes", focusing on material satisfaction and sensory enjoyment. In the world of the living, one should delight oneself with the finest things.

"I Turn the Carriage and Set Off" then turns to "establishing oneself".

I turn the carriage and set off,
Journeying along the endless long road.
Looking around, all is vast and boundless,
The east wind sways the hundred grasses.
Nothing I meet is what it used to be,
How can I not grow old quickly?
Prosperity and decline each have their time,
It is a pity not to establish oneself early.
Man's life is not like metal and stone,
How can he live to a great old age?
Swiftly we transform into other things,
Only glory and fame are to be treasured.

The poet also recognizes the brevity of life ("Nothing I meet is what it used to be, how can I not grow old quickly?"), but draws the conclusion that since we are destined to fade away, we must leave traces that can be eternal.

If "The Years of Life Reach Not One Hundred" represents withdrawing from social ethics and focusing on individual feelings, "I Turn the Carriage and Set Off" hopes to resist nothingness through socially recognized achievements. It believes that the value of life lies not in present enjoyment, but in the spirit's transcendence of time — being recorded in history and living in the memory of later generations.

We can see that even "establishing oneself" is motivated not by the call of the country and the times, but by the individual's deep anxiety about their own disappearance. The poet is not "establishing himself" for the country, but leaving traces for himself to resist time.

In The Book of Songs, love was classified into social order, but the perspective of Nineteen Old Poems is completely individualized. Even "achieving merit, virtue, and words" has been stripped of the grand cloak of social ethics and becomes an instinctive means for individuals to resist nothingness.

Beyond "fame and fortune" and "carpe diem", "The Eastern Wall is High and Long" proposes a third prescription — love and beauty, the most gentle and instinctive antidote.

The eastern wall is high and long,
Winding and stretching endlessly.
Whirlwinds rise from the ground,
Autumn grasses turn green and lush.
The four seasons change in turn,
How quickly the year draws to a close!
The morning wind bears a bitter heart,
The cricket laments its brief life.
Cast off your restraints and free your emotions,
Why bind yourself in fetters?
Yan and Zhao produce many beautiful women,
The fairest have faces like jade.
Dressed in silk robes and gowns,
They sit by the window playing clear melodies.
How sad the music sounds!
The strings are tight, the bridge pressed close.
My heart races as I adjust my sash,
I hesitate and linger, lost in thought.
I wish we could be a pair of swallows,
Carrying mud to build our nest in your house.

In the first half of the poem, the poet places himself in a cold environment: "Whirlwinds rise from the ground, autumn grasses turn green and lush. The four seasons change in turn, how quickly the year draws to a close!" — the rapid changes of the four seasons reinforce the destructive power of time. "The morning wind bears a bitter heart, the cricket laments its brief life" — the poet projects his own anxiety about "rapid decay" onto the cricket. Faced with this anxiety both internal and external, the poet actively cries out the declaration of liberation: "Cast off your restraints and free your emotions, why bind yourself in fetters?" He negates self-restraint and opens the door to the pursuit of emotions.

The poet projects this liberated emotion onto the beautiful woman "with a face like jade". He hopes "we could be a pair of swallows, carrying mud to build our nest in your house". In the torrent of time, to create a temporary, shared "eternity" for two individuals.

Even though love may be fragile, it remains the most instinctive means for humans to resist nothingness. It is the third and gentlest attempt after the failure of "carpe diem" and "establishing fame".

Although only "The Eastern Wall is High and Long" among the nineteen poems explicitly writes about "feeling the brevity of life and thus hoping to pursue love and beauty, to seek emotional connection", this is indeed the most instinctive thought humans have when facing the brevity of life. I think this is why there are so many love poems in Nineteen Old Poems, which constantly speaks of the transience of life.

Now let's talk about "The Departed Are More Distant Daily":

The departed grow more distant each day,
The new arrivals draw closer each day.
I leave the city gate and look straight ahead,
Only to see mounds and graves.
Ancient tombs have been plowed into fields,
Pines and cypresses cut down for firewood.
The white poplars sigh in the sad wind,
Their rustling sound kills the heart with sorrow.
I long to return to my hometown,
But there is no way to go back.

The opening two lines, "The departed grow more distant each day, the new arrivals draw closer each day" — this is the cruelest conclusion of the entire poem: the relationship between the dead and the living diminishes (grows distant), while the connection between newcomers and the world increases (grows closer). People are busy welcoming new intimacies and new lives, with no time to care about the departed individuals.

The poet comes to the cemetery again (similar to "I Ride My Chariot from the Upper East Gate"), but this time what he sees is not the white poplars for people to pay their respects to, but a scene of destruction:

"Ancient tombs have been plowed into fields, pines and cypresses cut down for firewood." Historical traces are completely erased. Even the resting places of the dead have been turned into farmland to nourish new lives. Those pines and cypresses that symbolized eternity have been cut down and become fuel for the living. This is more terrifying than seeing the graves themselves.

The poet is "killed with sorrow" by the scene before him, and thus has the impulse to "return" — wanting to go back to the warm "hometown" and escape this land of death. However, he painfully discovers "there is no way to go back". This may be for geographical reasons: the hometown has fallen or the road is blocked. It may also be philosophical: life itself is a sojourn, and there is no real hometown to return to.

"The Departed Are More Distant Daily" is very special. All the others start with external things, first describing scenery and then entering the theme. But this one puts the conclusion at the beginning. "The departed grow more distant each day, the new arrivals draw closer each day" is not an observed phenomenon, but a philosophical conclusion that has been deeply considered and internalized by the poet. The poet has seen through the inevitability of "forgetfulness" too thoroughly and too despairingly, so he does not need external things to trigger this sigh — it has already become a constant, indelible background in his heart.

The poet went out with the conclusion of "forgetfulness" in mind. He went to the cemetery not to find sorrow, but to confirm this conclusion — and as a result, the cruel scene of "ancient tombs have been plowed into fields, pines and cypresses cut down for firewood" perfectly proved the accuracy of "the departed grow more distant each day".

This is precisely the greatness of Nineteen Old Poems — it refuses to judge what is "right" or "wrong" like a moral textbook, but presents all possibilities equally: fame and fortune, carpe diem, love, despair. It shows us the true inner world of scholars in the Eastern Han Dynasty — they were all fighting the same enemy: time and death. They just chose different weapons.

Nineteen Old Poems acknowledges the complexity of the human heart: the same person may want to "wander by candle light" at one moment and "establish oneself" at another. It does not create utopias nor promise salvation. The themes it explores — birth, old age, illness, death, the passage of time — are universal, transcending the specific historical context of the Eastern Han Dynasty, and thus possess eternal appeal.

Textbooks hope we will be faithful to one partner for life and domesticate love into a part of social order. But we should not only read those two "safe" love poems. Instead, we should read all nineteen poems in their entirety to understand why scholars two thousand years ago wrote so many poems about love in the face of death and nothingness.
