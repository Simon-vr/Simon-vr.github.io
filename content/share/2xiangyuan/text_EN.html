I've never paid much attention to writings on morality, but this article hits the nail on the head with one point: courage is indeed a privilege of the nobility. This status endows courage with a power that surpasses all other virtues.

To embody any virtue, or to strive to be a good person, three fundamental elements are indispensable: goodwill, wisdom and courage – the so-called "Three Universal Virtues" in Confucianism.

These three qualities, like the three primary colors in art or the four fundamental forces in physics, form the bedrock of all virtues; they are the virtues among virtues, and none can be converted into another. Not a single one of them is dispensable for doing a good deed.

Goodwill dictates your desire to do good. Without goodwill, wisdom and courage alone make one a formidable yet unscrupulous hero.

Wisdom shows you how to do good. Without wisdom, good intentions lead to bad outcomes, making one a reckless fool.

Courage empowers you to act on doing good, even at the cost of sacrifice and responsibility at times. If a person has a kind heart and can weigh the pros and cons clearly, yet lacks the courage to stand up for what is right, they are a hypocritical conformist.

A hypocritical conformist is often full of goodwill, with an impeccable reputation, gentle and respectful in interactions, never falling out with others – seemingly a paragon of a "good person". They are also wise, with exceptional social skills. They see through human frailties and know how to stay out of trouble and curry favor with both sides to gain social praise and personal safety. Yet they lack courage: they dare not uphold the truth, dare not condemn evil, and always choose to go with the flow and compromise in the face of right and wrong.

"One can find no fault to accuse them of, no flaw to criticize. They follow the vulgar ways and conform to a corrupt world. They appear loyal and trustworthy in their conduct, and upright and incorruptible in their deeds. Everyone likes them, and they think highly of themselves, yet they can never attain the way of Yao and Shun." — Mencius

They are invulnerable to criticism: you want to blame them, yet find no specific fault; you want to reproach them, yet they act perfectly.

They are obsequiously ingratiating to the world, seeming loyal, trustworthy and incorruptible, and everyone likes them – yet they can never touch the true truth.

They blur the line between right and wrong. True virtue has an edge, but the hypocritical conformist turns morality into a form of "smooth sophistication".

They erode justice. When wrongdoing occurs, they dissuade those who dare to resist in the name of "harmony above all else". They cloak their indulgence of evil under the guise of goodwill.

First and foremost, they lack courage, and in turn, misapply their wisdom and emasculate their goodwill. In the end, goodwill rots into philistinism, and wisdom degenerates into cynicism – the hypocritical conformist is the ultimate form of such decay.

Courage is of such great importance that it is directly defined as the most essential quality, one of the Four Cardinal Virtues, in both Aristotelian ethics and Stoicism.

Furthermore, courage is also the hardest virtue to acquire and cultivate in our daily lives. It is actually easy for a person to gain goodwill, for social conditioning teaches people to be kind; even a wicked person knows what is good and what is bad. Acquiring wisdom is not all that difficult either, for it can be gained from production and practice. A wise person often means a more sophisticated way of acting and higher productivity, which in turn may lead to a higher social status – this is also a positive incentive.

But courage is different. More often than not, courage is not encouraged by social conditioning; it may even require breaking the unspoken rules of society, with the accompanying responsibility and cost. For example, if you see a thief on the street and step forward to catch them, you run the risk of being stabbed in their resistance. If you misjudge the situation and blame an innocent person by mistake, you also have to apologize and compensate. Yet if you pretend not to see, you suffer no harm at all. Even the best foreseeable outcome of catching the thief is nothing more than the applause of onlookers and a reward from the victim that does not exceed their potential losses.

This kind of courage is not the fearlessness of injury or death – the reckless attitude of "eat to live, and die if so" is not courage. Even drinking a bowl of strong liquor to be a suicide soldier who charges into battle, seizes the enemy's flag and beheads their generals is only a small part of courage. Courage means choosing to act, knowing full well that you are not obliged to do so, that acting will bring cost and responsibility, and that the short-term gains are even a negative expectation compared to the risks.

So why is goodness without courage called "hypocritical conformism"?

Because, to put it bluntly, a courageous person is often born into a wealthy and noble family from an early age. Thus, they are free from excessive social conditioning (or rather, the nobility has its own esoteric moral code), and have abundant capital to experiment and bear the consequences, giving them the chance to cultivate courage. It can even be said that courage is almost an exclusive virtue of the nobility. (This does not mean, of course, that all nobles are courageous, nor that there are no courageous commoners.)

People from humble backgrounds, however, often lack courage, and thus their goodness is naturally the "hypocritical conformism" of the common folk.
