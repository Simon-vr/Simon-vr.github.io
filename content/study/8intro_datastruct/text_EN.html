Learning Path

  • I think Data Structures and Algorithms is probably the first major field every computer science freshman encounters. At least for me personally, it felt like the biggest and only thing I saw after getting started: like a mountain standing right in front of me, the only peak to climb with no other choices. You'll hear ACMers talking about how many problem sets they've finished on Luogu, how many Accepted and Exceeded solutions they have; job seekers debating whether they can solve medium or hard problems on LeetCode; countless Bilibili creators selling paid or free courses; countless Indian tutors online teaching knapsack problems and trapping rain water; and legendary Codeforces top programmers admired on online forums... But what I want to say is, looking back now, computer science is so much more than this. If you feel tired or bored of it, step out of this circle and explore what truly interests you. Practicing algorithm problems may indeed be a necessary step for building foundations and job hunting, but it shouldn't be deified, taken as the sole goal, or make you think you can't handle other fields just because you struggle with algorithm problems—this is far from the truth. Especially now that LLMs have completely outperformed humans, unless it's a personal hobby, investing extra ambition in tricky algorithm problems is not worthwhile.
  • Since I spent a lot of time on this before, I'll organize it anyway—just in case someone wants to follow my path
  • I think by the time you take this course, most students will already understand nearly all the content. If you wrote and submitted every problem in your C and C++ courses on your own, you should already be familiar with arrays, linked lists, recursion, sorting, various string operations, and complexity analysis. For further learning, you can refer to:
  • Code Thoughts (Code Sui Xiang Lu), the best tutorial I've ever found! The creator from Northeast China, Ka'er, explains concepts in a simple and in-depth way like no one else. I'm proud to say I mastered almost all the content (excluding graph theory at the time) during the 2024 winter vacation, only to forget most of it by junior year. However, his lessons on tree traversal and dynamic planning left a deep impression—when I later encountered graph search and reinforcement learning, everything felt familiar. In summary, this video series is a powerful tool for job hunting, systematically covering all core algorithms comprehensively.
  • Introduction to Algorithms (Thomas H. Cormen et al.): The classic "black book", world-renowned and needs no introduction. Its structure focuses more on algorithmic discussions, so beginners may find it easier to start with the "Algorithms" book below first, then revisit this one after mastering data structures—this will make the learning curve much smoother. Recommended chapters: Chapters 15 and 16. Particular attention should be paid to the divide-and-conquer concept, as many techniques evolve from it and are truly fascinating.
  • Algorithms (4th Edition, Robert Sedgewick, Kevin Wayne): An excellent introductory book! Compared to Introduction to Algorithms, it's far more beginner-friendly for implementing your own data structures. Its explanation of red-black trees is the best I've ever seen—thoroughly clarifying the intuitive logic behind them. The only downside is that it uses Java, which I wasn't very familiar with.
  • LeetCode: I solved about 100 problems in total, half medium. Looking back, I shouldn't have spent so much time on it—honestly, Sisyphus only feels happy for a split second when seeing "Accepted", and suffers the rest of the time. I'd rather have spent that time playing with open-source projects or similar things. Even if it didn't bring tangible gains, at least it would have been more fun.
  • Codeforces / Luogu: Recommended for those interested in competitive programming
