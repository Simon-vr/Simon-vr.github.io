I finished reading White Nights in bed at noon. I’d been picking at it for a long time, but it’s really just a tiny, tiny book. I owe thanks to J—her past advice let me read it calmly and objectively today, and truly take something from it. If I’d read it a year or two ago, I might have been more shaken, but I wouldn’t have thought about it the way I do now.

It’s a simple story of unrequited love. The narrator calls himself a dreamer, long sexually repressed. He meets Nastenka by chance, quickly pours all his feelings into her, and ends up disappointed. That’s all.

The dreamer’s problem isn’t loneliness, nor poverty. It’s something harder to put into words. He lives in society, but has no real place within it. He’s not a beggar, nor is he starving in the streets—but the whole of St. Petersburg seems to have no need for him. Put simply, within the entire social structure, he has no power, no position, no resources, no identity, no voice whatsoever. As a petty intellectual, a new city dweller in 19th-century St. Petersburg, he has no steady job, no clear future, little financial foundation, surviving only on translation and tutoring. He belongs to no system of power, no system of wealth, no stable community at all. He lives in St. Petersburg, but St. Petersburg does not need him.

I’ve felt this kind of pain deeply for a long time, so reading this little book felt deeply personal.

Every time I go back north, I rant to my few remaining friends about how terrible Sun Yat-sen University is, desperate to leave. But when I actually try to say what’s so wrong with this place, my exaggerated complaints can never convince me late at night. This isn’t a high school-style prison, after all—no one restricts what you do. It’s not a desert island either; every day, familiar faces you’ve never greeted sit 10 meters away from you. Every day there are classes to attend, assignments to hand in, exams to prepare for, endless deadlines of one kind or another.

But what’s it really like?

You don’t matter to anything. Most of what happens here doesn’t matter to you either. What I mean is, as an undergraduate in 2026, you’re nobody. No one cares about anything related to you. And I know full well no one is obligated to.

But even if you do nothing at all—if you just stand in the middle of the road, sit across from someone, walk past everyone, stare into their pupils—they won’t even lift their heads or nod. And no one feels ashamed of it, no slightest embarrassment at ignoring another person’s existence for a moment.

I’ve grown numb to it, and had to accept that everyone is a complete, closed atom. This place is too perfect, too complete to need anything filling it—not that there’s nothing to fill it with, but there’s simply nowhere to put anything.

When I read about the dreamer, I keep seeing that same feeling in him.

Many interpretations say he’s obsessed with fantasy. But fantasy never comes from nowhere. A person always loses something first, before they need fantasy. Someone who can find dignity, value, and place in the real world rarely stakes their entire life on dreams. Fantasy only becomes important when reality can’t offer those things.

The dreamer lives in total powerlessness for a long time. And in that state, people instinctively search for a space they can control. Sometimes that space is anime, sometimes religion, sometimes a grand narrative, sometimes a world built by idols and fans.

They look very different, but I increasingly think they’re essentially the same thing: power. Like animals claiming territory—declaring I am here, I support this, this is who I am—it’s fundamentally a form of power. Someone with no power in society can’t do that. When you’re disempowered in society, no one cares who you are. The perfect example: if you’re new and speak up at work or in public, people will only take it as provocation. Hey, I’m here, I want my own power in your territory!

So we’re all disempowered—undergraduates, poor citizens of St. Petersburg, all of us.

Looking at Nastenka this way, I don’t think her importance is really about love. She’s more like an entry point, one that lets the dreamer briefly escape his powerlessness. In reality, he can control nothing—not his future, not his fate, not how others see him. But in his relationship with Nastenka, he has a space of his own for the first time, his own power: he can be needed, and need someone in return; he can wait, and be waited for; he can even define himself as noble, devoted, kind, self-sacrificing.

None of these identities have a place in the real world, because the real world doesn’t care who he is. But over those four nights, it all becomes real. So I keep thinking he didn’t love Nastenka herself, not entirely. What he loved was the version of himself that finally had power.

From this angle, I can’t even mock his desperation at the end. Many would say it’s childish to stake his whole life on love—but if someone has lost all power, almost everything else to hold onto, what else is left to stake?

When a person can’t find their place in reality, they condense their whole life onto one thing. It could be love, an ideal, a faith, anything in a fantasy world. Because nowhere else can they prove they ever truly existed.

And that’s exactly what struck me most about White Nights. The dreamer probably knew all along it was fragile. But when reality can no longer convince a person to keep investing in it, even the weakest illusion becomes priceless.
