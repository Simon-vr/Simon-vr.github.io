Last night, I finished the last few pages huddled under my blanket. A shiver ran through me, making sleep impossible. My mind raced with chaotic, relentless thoughts—I wanted to get up and write them down, but morning would come too soon, so I didn't. Instead, I forced myself to formulate a title in the dark, lest I forget everything by dawn. What is this book about? Dignity—the narrator spends the longest passages reminiscing and elaborating on this; Miss Kenton's love—the hidden thread running through the present-day narrative; three-quarters through, I thought the theme might be: how so-called "dignity" suppresses the self, creating an irreconcilable tension between dignity and emotional understanding. I had much to say on this, yet it didn't feel truly devastating. Then I read the final chapters—the narrator's conversation with villagers (here, for the first time, the book hooked me, made me bite, made me feel the author's narrative sorcery), the reunion with Miss Kenton, the exchange with the stranger on the bench—suddenly the path turned, the novel's suppressed tension released rapidly (honestly, in earlier sections I had ignored this tension embedded in the prose, taking it for granted). I exhaled. A looming conviction was confirmed—yet the final sentence pulled everything back, as if nothing had changed. So the theme, I believe, has nothing to do with dignity or love. They are merely vehicles for life's self-suppression, self-deception, self-consolation—though the choice of vehicles is not meaningless; in their absurdity I found shadows of myself, recalled past events.

How a person describes something often reveals more than hearing their opinions directly. After all, expressing opinions easily allows for intentional or unintentional evasion or self-deception, while describing something naturally embeds one's stance in the angle of storytelling. Moreover, my mind remains chaotic. Allow me to retrace the narrative—thus the theme will become clearer.

The story unfolds as a first-person diary, set roughly in the 1950s or 60s. It documents the narrator's four-day journey to visit his former colleague, housekeeper Miss Kenton. Yet within the diary, the bulk of space is devoted to recalling the past, arguing his values (repeatedly illustrating two questions: What is dignity? What makes a great butler?), culminating in the meeting with Miss Kenton—the climax, then the end.

Before delving deeper, I should introduce the background, without which one cannot understand the narrator's life:

The narrator is the butler of Darlington Hall. Darlington Hall is the estate of Lord Darlington, one of Britain's more prestigious old nobles, and the butler stands at the apex of the household management system: overseeing dozens of male and female servants, under-butlers. Thus, the narrator's position represents the pinnacle of the butler hierarchy, the highest achievement of the profession, which he occupied throughout the 1930s—the primary period of the novel's recollections.

The events of the 1920s and 30s can be summarized thus: Lord Darlington was weak and kind-hearted, lacking political acumen, but driven by the conscience and tradition of aristocratic statesmanship, he constantly hosted gatherings at his estate, hoping to play his historical role. What role? To alleviate Germany's reparations under the post-WWI Treaty of Versailles. Why dedicate himself to this? Because his lordship was an old aristocrat, and old aristocrats valued noblesse oblige: having defeated your enemy, you help him up. Moreover, his lordship was kind and wished to spare German civilians suffering. So he shuttled between capitals, organizing meetings between French and German officials, seeking development for Germany. Did it succeed? Obviously not. To help Germany, in the 1930s his lordship grew close to Nazi diplomats, and due to his hesitant, weak character, showed poor judgment of Nazi intentions. After WWII, his lordship was branded a traitor, condemned by the press, and died in despair. The Darlington line declined; Darlington Hall was sold to an American.

From the macro perspective, the entire British aristocracy rapidly decayed: ancestral estates sold, aristocratic politics yielding to modern democracy, moral governance yielding to expert governance—and, most relevant here: the professional ethos of the aristocratic butler reduced to a toy for American millionaires to flaunt their taste.

Background complete. Shall we begin the story? I think we might wait. There is more to say.

The "butler" as an appendage of the aristocratic class—the narrator clearly stands at the vortex of this historical decline. How he views this history, or how he views his own earlier career, becomes crucial.

How does he view it? As first-person narration, the narrator maintains throughout his journey an aristocratic, rigid, restrained, or one might say classical tone in describing his memories and observations. Honestly, it leaves one breathless, as if a pedantic old butler were droning on before you. The diary's themes, its argumentative sections, address only two subjects:

What is dignity? The narrator: Dignity is restraint. This restraint manifests as: the butler suppressing all impulse and emotion for his work (serving his employer); the noble maintaining conscience and composure for great causes. (Half the book's length, arguing this incessantly.)

What makes a great butler? The narrator: First, dignity (as above); second, serving an employer with great purposes (described as the axle around which everything rotates).

Here, cracks begin to show. Why is he so obsessed with these two questions? Arguing back and forth, citing examples, examining every detail of memory? Initially, I didn't realize this was a problem. The calm, rigorous prose gave a sense of security: of course such a watertight butler should have clear consciousness of his mission.

Yet as the argument progresses, you feel this security (I wonder if "security" confuses—should I borrow "consistency" from mathematical proof? The consistency of the narrator's first-person discourse) slowly collapsing, bit by bit revealing the truth beneath, which is precisely the real background described above.

Initially, his consciousness aligns with fact: Recalling his father's butler principles; Recalling the butlers' fireside conversations; Recalling his lordship's elegance and bearing; Affirming his current American employer as a true gentleman; Repeatedly emphasizing that this journey aims only to recruit Miss Kenton's help.

Later, in memory, cracks appear between consciousness and fact: An American politician at a banquet calling his lordship an amateur who understands nothing; Some butlers serving not old nobles but businessmen, whom the narrator struggles to deem great; Insisting his conversations with Miss Kenton were purely professional, yet picking up her letters, parsing every sentence.

Gradually, memory grows clamorous: Repeatedly calling his lordship good, yet recalling he dismissed all Jewish employees; His lordship's nephew slamming the table, trying to tell the narrator his lordship's cause was futile, that he was being used; Recalling Miss Kenton, proposed to, returning to the house, weeping behind a door. ......

Finally, memory's colors fade. He returns to the real world, received as nobility by eager villagers. Here, his ideology splits completely from reality; the calm discourse begins to vibrate: reading this, I finally understood—the previous discourse on dignity, on greatness, was nothing but the narrator deceiving himself, thoroughly deceiving himself. He needs to repeatedly prove greatness precisely because he vaguely senses that structure of meaning loosening. Lord Darlington's cause did not change history; the narrator's loyalty may not have participated in any grand process. If the employer is not great, then the logic of "serving greatness" loses its foundation. So he can only define dignity with greater force.

In fact, behind the seemingly calm argument lies the brutal fact: his life is meaningless. His lordship's life is meaningless.

And he clearly understood Miss Kenton's love, yet never accepted it, pursuing his dignity and meaning. He clearly understood Miss Kenton's failed marriage, her repeated departures, yet never admitted to himself that he still cared for her.

Now, beginning to understand everything is meaningless (though not admitting it), the journey's true purpose shatters for the reader: his instinct (love from beyond the lie, authentic love) drives him to meet Miss Kenton, hoping she is well.

The interesting point arrives: they converse pleasantly, yet Miss Kenton denies her marriage has failed, insists she still loves her husband.

Miss Kenton deceives herself as well. She admits to past wavering, then quickly returns herself to the narrative of marriage; the narrator, after a moment's disorientation, returns to his professional identity, resuming classical, obscure diction. No one truly breaks free. His lordship lives in the illusion of aristocratic bearing; Miss Kenton in the narrative of family; the villagers in the narrative of democratic politics. Everyone has a net; everyone maintains order within it.

Finally, at sunset's edge, the narrator weeps before the stranger on the bench (in this moment, he alone breaks free from his lie)—and now, the remains of the day.

It demonstrates such a state: over long years, one weaves a language to explain the world, lodging oneself firmly within. That language need not be entirely false; it may even contain sincerity, yet it quietly excludes facts threatening stability. When the remains of the day arrive, certain cracks appear; we briefly see the net's outline, yet still choose to continue living inside.

At the book's end, the narrator decides to practice jokes, to better serve his employer. Everything seems to return to the origin. Yet the reader knows: those cracks truly exist. Only, one does not immediately leave one's net upon seeing the cracks. We often fail to realize we are inside; even realizing, we may not extract ourselves.

And what of dignity, discussed at such length?

Borrowing the narrator's words to the doctor: "Dignity is not undressing before others." The doctor pauses, stunned. In that instant, the net seems to sway slightly, yet does not break.

What clothes? What belt?

Do not remove your net.

The remains of the day; as light withdraws, we realize we have been using language to shield ourselves from dusk. And that net was never imposed by others—we wove it with our own hands.
