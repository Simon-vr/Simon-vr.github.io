I loved this film deeply, and I was left wanting more long after it ended. It shows how ordinary people can find poetry and happiness in the monotony of daily life. In many ways, Paterson’s life is my ideal: a small house to myself, a partner who shares my values and outlook on life, a dog, a garage, and a little basement retreat. One person’s regular job is enough to support both of them. They work five days a week, finish at four or five in the afternoon, spend every evening chatting with friends at the bar, and on weekends watch movies and bake cupcakes — even if they’re only just getting by. Even so, it is far better than working at a big tech company, living in a hive-like apartment, and belonging to a family constantly on edge, filled with inexplicable anxiety and worry.

I remember once after a mock exam in my senior year of high school, I felt as if I’d just left a battlefield. Sitting by the windowsill, I said to a friend: “Honestly, I think being a security guard or a librarian is in some ways the ideal job in modern society — no cutthroat competition, and so much free time.” Looking back, that idea is hardly realistic, but with all the things Paterson has, what’s wrong with being a bus driver?

Of course, the biggest difference-maker here is having a thoughtful and understanding wife like the one in the film. Otherwise, living in such a spacious house, finishing work so early, and writing poetry alone would probably feel quite lonely. I have to say, Laura is exactly my image of the perfect Latin woman: a passionate personality, tanned skin, long black curly hair, and deep eyes. Paterson truly is blessed.

In the film, Paterson is already a run-down city. Its shabby roads, decaying infrastructure, and a growing Black population all point to its dire conditions. When I looked up the city’s current situation, I was shocked to come across this article. Considering the city’s poetic spirit and left-wing tradition, perhaps it is not so surprising after all.
