Basic Information

  • Class Schedule

    • First semester of freshman year: Mathematical Analysis I
    • Second semester of freshman year: Mathematical Analysis II

  • Course & Exam Difficulty

    • 5 out of 5 difficulty rating - extremely difficult. If you want to maintain your GPA, don't let your guard down for a second.
    • Personally think this course is only suitable for freshmen - you need the momentum from high school to grit your teeth and work through it slowly.

  • Textbook Evaluation

    • Concise Course in Mathematical Analysis
    • 1 out of 5 rating - absolutely terrible. The anti-self-learning seal is too strong: the book has rigorous theorems and proofs, but won't teach you the mathematical intuition behind them. Overall it can only be used as a reference book. This textbook does an excellent job of being "concise", but whether it's actually "clear" or can be called a "course" seems to be something the author doesn't care about. So I suggest renaming this book to "Mathematical Analysis Theorems and Exercises Quick Reference Manual" - in that regard, it excels.

  • Course Evaluation

    • Luo Zhihong/Tan Zhijun - pretty standard, nothing special.

Study Route

  • This course is positioned as "Advanced Mathematics Pro". In terms of exam content, it adds more proof problems on top of advanced mathematics. But from the classroom experience, it feels no different from advanced mathematics.
  • Abroad, this course is generally called "Calculus"/"Advanced Calculus"/"Real Analysis" - you can look for more related materials.
  • After taking this course, I started to feel that "mathematics" and "calculation-ology" are two different things. What we learned as "mathematics" in middle school should probably be called "calculation-ology" - how to quantitatively analyze a problem, calculate the result, and get the score. Of course, the actual requirements in this course are nothing more than "calculation-ology" - if you can master various calculus calculations, memorize convergence and series proofs, and understand various definitions and theorems you'll never use in eight lifetimes, then you can get pretty good scores on the final exam. It's just that this course becomes neither "mathematical" nor "analytical", and things start to get boring.
  • So what's the difference between mathematics and calculation-ology? In my shallow understanding, mathematics should be an intuition, or philosophy - it's a perspective, no different from perspectives in any other field. Calculation is just support for this perspective/intuition, far from being the perspective/intuition itself, and certainly cannot reveal the worldview behind the perspective. This intuition is reflected in the proofs of various theorems: before you understand the intuition, you'll think "This needs proof?" or "What's the use of proving this?", but after understanding the intuition behind it, you'll feel everything is natural (this perspective should naturally be placed here - as for the proof and specific implementation, that's calculation-ology's business...)
  • I strongly recommend Teiji Takagi's "Introduction to Analysis". I think its logic is very smooth, the proof processes are written extremely clearly. More importantly, if you start from the beginning, you won't encounter any obstacles from prerequisite knowledge. After reading each chapter, you'll feel that the previous knowledge (perspectives) are useful, clear, and intuitive. Reading this book feels like knowledge flows through your brain like water and flows right back out. The order of this book differs from the course syllabus (obviously this book is more scientific), but if you master its content, even concepts not mentioned in the textbook become very easy to understand. After reading, you just feel refreshed - reading the "Concise Course" practically gives you a heart attack.
  • This book has a matching course: Professor Qi Zhenyu's "Calculus" course from National Taiwan University (link). I highly recommend this course - Qi Zhenyu is the most outstanding math teacher I've ever seen, bar none. Before listening to his classes, I had never attended a real mathematics course. After listening to his classes, I haven't seen any mathematics course that can compare. Additionally, the blackboard writing in this course is in English, but Professor Qi speaks in Chinese, which can serve as a transition for listening to Indian guys explaining algorithms other English courses later.
  • If you have finals coming up, please ignore everything above and quickly work through the end-of-chapter problems in the "Concise Course". If you can't understand them, at least memorize the answers.

  • My recollection of the 2024 real exam:
    • Mathematical Analysis I: First time here, all final exam questions were from homework! Every single question was from homework! If you understand every homework problem, you're good.
    • Mathematical Analysis II: Final exam had no homework questions, but I remember many guys scored around 90, so no need to panic. Still use end-of-chapter exercises as the outline for review - if you review all the points tested in the exercises, there shouldn't be major problems.
    • There will be proof questions, but they generally won't be too difficult.
