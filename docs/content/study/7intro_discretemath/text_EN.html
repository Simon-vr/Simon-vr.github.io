Basic Info

  • Offered: Fall of sophomore year
  • Difficulty: 3/5, much easier than linear algebra and mathematical analysis
  • Exam difficulty: 3/5, lots of picky details to review
  • Textbook Review
    • Foundations of Discrete Mathematics (Zhou Xiaocong & Qiao Haiyan, Tsinghua University Press)
    • Never really read it—slides were enough. Still, compared with the slides the book gives fuller examples and explanations; if you skip class, flipping through it fills the gaps nicely. Both authors are from SYSU and actually teach this course; they also designed the slides and syllabus. Honestly, they’ve poured heart and soul into the course, especially Prof. Zhou—textbook, assignments, slides, sample code, Q&A, the works. How many teachers still do that these days?
  • Course Review
    • Liu Yongmei, a plump middle-aged woman, takes teaching seriously. Nothing particularly inspiring, but nothing to complain about either.

Learning Path

  • After finishing the course I still didn’t have a coherent picture of discrete math (maybe my math background is just weak). It felt like a grab-bag of topics—sure, everything is “discrete,” no continuous functions or analysis, but the discrete bits don’t seem related. Roughly four independent blocks:
    • Logic
      • Propositions, equivalences, inference, formal proofs, that sort of thing.
      • Pretty fiddly, and—get this—the exact same material is repeated next semester in the required AI course! Two required courses with zero coordination, so we waste two months rehashing identical content—though, to be fair, even new content wouldn’t be listened to. Those two months felt absurd: everyone knew it was pointless, no one reminded the lecturer, we all kept our heads down and muddled through. No need to restate the obvious—our undergraduate education is already rotten. Anyway, I digress.
      • Personally, learning formal proofs didn’t make me any better at persuading people, but it maybe trained me to ask what the premises are, what an equivalent statement might be, what exactly we’re trying to prove. Not a total loss. Still, I’ve never used any of it in later code or projects.
    • Sets / Relations / Functions
      • Three chapters, yet there’s a clear progression: in the world of sets an element has only one attribute—membership—so we talk union, intersection, complement, power set, the usual. With relations we introduce ordered pairs, then partial orders, equivalences, etc.; essentially we draw a directed graph on an unordered set. You can also view a relation as a set of ordered pairs and discuss reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity inside that set—another layer. Functions add the attribute of “image,” letting us link two sets; of course a function is just a special kind of relation: a directed edge that enforces a mapping, yet another layer.
      • This helps a bit when you later meet relational databases, but only a bit.
      • If you elect “Algebraic Structures” you’ll be tortured by this material for another whole semester—pure agony. Unless you’re a math masochist, stay away.
    • Counting & Combinatorics
      • High-school permutations & combinations you still half-remember sophomore year. Not much new.
    • Graphs & Trees
      • Mainly tedious definitions, proofs, and theorems. Planar graphs, Eulerian graphs, all sorts of random stuff—one even showed up on the final, absolutely nauseating.
      • For actual practice you’ll see more in data-structures and algorithms; at the pure-math level it feels like reading scripture—no idea how or where to apply these concepts.
  • Every section had an assignment on Xuexitong; looking back, it was one of the most assignment-heavy major courses, but the tasks make decent review material for the final.
  • We got the occasional in-class quiz; it technically counts toward the grade, but you can just wing it.
